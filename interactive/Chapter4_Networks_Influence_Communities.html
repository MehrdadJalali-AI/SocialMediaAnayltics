Social Network Analytics I — Influence, Communities & Homophily

So far we focused on what people say. Today we look at who is connected with whom. Social media is also a network of users, replies, mentions and reposts — and its structure shapes who is seen, who is heard, and which groups form. Network results depend on how we build the network. Design first, then measure.

Section 2

Five design decisions before any metric

Edges and attributes

Directed (A → B): mentions, reposts, follows · undirected (A — B): co-authors, co-members, friends.
Attributes: weight (12 mentions in March) · type (reply, mention, repost) · rank (best friend, colleague) · time (needed in Ch 5).

Special structures

Tree — one parent per node: a reply thread.
DAG — directed, no cycles: citations, repost cascades.

Every metric inherits these decisions.EV mention network: nodes = pseudonymised users · edge = A mentions B · directed · weight = number of mentions · window = 1–14 March 2026 · drop edges with weight 1? — a choice you must report.

Section 4

Storing social graphs — and the tools

Question: who do Alice’s contacts mention? In SQL every extra hop needs one more join; in a graph database you simply follow edges.

-- SQL: who do Alice's contacts mention?
SELECT m2.target FROM mentions m1
JOIN mentions m2 ON m1.target = m2.source
WHERE m1.source = 'alice';
// Cypher (Neo4j): the same question
MATCH (a:User {id:"alice"})-[:MENTIONS]->()-[:MENTIONS]->(t)
RETURN DISTINCT t
{ "_id": "alice", "mentions": ["bob", "carla"], "lang": "de" }
// flexible, but not built for multi-hop traversal

Section 6

Traversal: BFS, DFS and shortest paths

Breadth-first search (BFS)

Level by level: direct friends, then friends of friends. Finds shortest paths in unweighted graphs.

Depth-first search (DFS)

Follows one path as deep as possible, then backtracks. Useful for components and deep chains.

Animate a traversal

On average, any two members are steps apart. For weighted graphs, Dijkstra’s algorithm finds the shortest path when edges have costs. BFS is also how information reaches friends of friends — Chapter 5 builds on it.

Signals of influence

receives many mentions or replies · is reposted often (Ch 5) · sits between communities · is cited by other important users · changes what others post afterwards

Why a high score can mislead

Activity ≠ influence (mentioning everyone = high out-degree) · bots inflate degree and PageRank · popularity ≠ persuasion · missing data — API samples hide part of the network

Centrality measures position — influence needs evidence of effect.

Section 9 · Part C

Community detection with Louvain

Louvain maximises modularity: many ties inside groups, few between them. The resolution parameter decides how many communities you get — a choice, not a fact.

Section 10

Homophily: birds of a feather

Homophily: similar nodes are more likely to be connected (language, region, political view, interests, profession, age group).

Selection (homophily)

Similar people choose to connect. Two EV owners follow each other because both own an EV.

Social influence (contagion)

Connected people become similar. After following an EV owner, a user starts posting positively about EVs.

A single network snapshot cannot tell them apart — you need data over time.
Assortativity on the karate club

−1 = connect to different · 0 = random · +1 = connect to similar

Section 11 · Part D

Link prediction and node classification

Who will connect next? Click two members
Top unconnected pairs by Adamic–Adar

Common neighbours — how many friends do they share? · Jaccard — shared ÷ all friends of both · Adamic–Adar — shared friends, rare friends count more (Σ 1/log degree). Structure predicts ties — but not whether people want them.

Node classification: labels from neighbours

We know the group of only the two leaders (0 and 33). Predict everyone else from the network alone.

Used for bot vs. human detection, topic communities and user roles. Careful: predicting labels from friends can reveal private attributes (GDPR Art. 9).
