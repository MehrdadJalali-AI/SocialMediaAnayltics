Foundations of Social Media Analytics & Sentiment Analysis

Every day, billions of people post, comment, like and share. Together these traces show what people think, how they feel, and how ideas move between them. This chapter gives you the foundation: what the field is, what social media data looks like, why it is hard, and how to work with it responsibly. Section 2

Why careful social media analysis matters

Social media gives fast answers — sometimes to the wrong question. Click each card to see what goes wrong without care.

Misread opinions
Sarcasm and negation flip the meaning: “Great, another delay” is a complaint, not praise.
Unrepresentative samples
The loudest users of one platform are not “the public”. Students tweeting about a university policy may be far more vocal than everyone else.
Inflated signals
Bots, spam and duplicates make a topic look bigger. One person with many bot accounts can turn a small issue into a “major public concern”.
Privacy harm
Quoting or profiling identifiable users can hurt real people and break GDPR — even if the post is public.

Section 3

What is social media analytics — and sentiment analysis?

Social media analytics (SMA)

The process of collecting, preparing, analysing and interpreting social media data to understand people, opinions and interactions. It combines NLP, machine learning, statistics, visualization and network science.

Sentiment analysis (SA)

The automatic identification of opinions, attitudes and evaluations in text. The most common task is polarity classification: positive · neutral · negative.

TextLabelWhy
“This course is really useful.”positiveclear positive evaluation
“The course starts at 9:30.”neutrala fact, no evaluation
“The course is confusing and too fast.”negativeclear negative evaluation
🔍
Sentiment analysis sits inside social media analytics. SMA asks about topics, influence, engagement, spread and communities. SA zooms in on one question: is the opinion positive, neutral or negative — and how does that change?

Section 4

Why social media data is different

Social media text is not newspaper text. Click a property to see a real-style example.

⚡ Fast
A complaint about a delayed train can trend within an hour; topics change within hours.
💬 Informal
“omg this app is sooo bad 😭” — slang, creative spelling, emojis.
📢 Noisy
The same advert posted many times by bots or spam accounts; typos everywhere.
✂️ Short
“Not again!” — almost no information without context.
🌍 Multilingual
“Das Konzert was amazing!” — German and English in one post.
🕸 Networked
Posts reply to, mention and share others; popularity comes from the network.
🖼 Multimodal
A photo of a broken product with the caption “Very disappointed.”
🧭 Context-dependent
“Great job!” can be praise or sarcasm depending on the thread.

Section 8

Types of sentiment labels & levels of analysis

The most common setting: negative neutral positive. “Love the new update!” → positive · “The event starts at 8:00.” → neutral · “This app keeps crashing.” → negative.

Fine-grained sentiment, similar to 1–5-star ratings: very negative · negative · neutral · positive · very positive. More detail, but annotators disagree more on neighbouring classes.

LabelMeaningExample
mixedpositive and negative together“Good design but bad battery.”
unknownannotators could not decide“Well, that happened.”
not relevantoff-topic, spam or advertising“Click here for free coins”
  • Who created the labels — humans or a model?
  • Are the classes balanced?
  • Do annotators agree?
A label set is a design decision, not a fact.

Section 9

Sentiment, emotion and stance

“I am worried about electric cars, but I fully support the new subsidy policy.”

Sentiment

Is the evaluation positive, neutral or negative?
Labels: positive · neutral · negative · mixed

Output: mixed

Emotion

Which feeling is expressed?
Labels: joy · anger · fear · sadness · surprise · disgust

Output: fear (worry)

Stance

Is the author in favour of or against a target?
Labels: favour · against · none (target: subsidy policy)

Output: favour

Same post, three questions, three different labels.Emotion is not sentiment, and stance always needs a target.
Activity 1.1 — label six posts about a city’s new e-scooter ban

Label each post for sentiment, emotion and stance (target: the scooter ban). Then compare with the sample answer. Disagreement on posts 4–6 is expected — that is why real datasets need annotation guidelines and agreement checks.

Section 11

Social networks: from interactions to a graph

Social network analytics studies relationships between social media entities as a graph: nodes (users, posts, hashtags, pages), edges (follows, mentions, replies, shares), a weight (how often) and a direction (who mentions whom). Chapters 4–5 answer: who is influential? who connects communities? how do communities form? do similar users connect (homophily)? how does information spread (diffusion)?

Interaction log → weighted edge list → directed graph
→
1 · Interaction log
2 · Weighted edge list
3 · Directed graph

Section 13

Ethics, privacy and GDPR

Responsible analytics starts before coding. Public ≠ free to use: public posts are still personal data under GDPR.

PrincipleThe riskWhat helps
Data minimisationCollecting full user profiles “just in case”.Keep only the fields your question needs (GDPR Art. 5(1)(c)).
PseudonymisationUsernames, quotes and screenshots can identify people.Replace user IDs, paraphrase quotes, report aggregates.
Platform rulesScraping can break terms of service and API licences.Use official APIs or published datasets; read the licence.
Sensitive inferenceInferring health, religion or political views from posts.Avoid special-category inference (Art. 9 GDPR) without a lawful basis and ethics approval.
Pseudonymise a post

Paste or edit a post. The tool replaces @handles with stable pseudonyms (USER_1, USER_2…), masks URLs and e-mail addresses, and flags quotes that could still be searched for.

Section 16

Theory summary

1 · Social media analytics

studies posts, users, engagement, topics and networks.

2 · Sentiment analysis

identifies opinions and attitudes in text.

3 · A post is more than text

text + metadata + engagement + network context.

4 · Labels and levels

positive, neutral, negative, mixed — per document, sentence or aspect.

5 · Social media text is hard

sarcasm, emojis, slang and missing context.

6 · Ethics, GDPR and bias

are part of the analytical design, not an add-on.
